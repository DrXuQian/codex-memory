The MoE result was 7.62–7.66 µs across 12 isolated trials; six full sweeps had a 7.66 µs median. Its grid is `1 × 256 × 8 = 2048` CTAs, exactly 16× the collective’s 128 CTAs. This winning configuration uses 256 threads/CTA.

The 153.7% figure is the requested normalization—compulsory bytes divided by time and 1.792 TB/s—but it is not HBM utilization. The warm 21 MB operand fits in the 5090’s L2, producing an effective 2.754 TB/s. A cache-flushed counter measurement was attempted, but Nsight Compute was blocked by `ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM`. The dense operand is likewise cache-resident.

PPU references:

- Tensor-core grouped GEMM: **20.74 µs / 37.5% HBM**
- SIMT `gemv_lowbit`: **22.27 µs / 34.1% HBM**
- Thus GEMV was **7.4% slower** despite the 16× CTA supply.

The 5090 therefore does not reproduce that ordering under the normalized compulsory-byte metric: its SIMT result appears to win decisively. But that cannot settle the PPU question—the result is L2-served, no 5090 tensor-core collective was measured, and this project already records configuration rankings inverting between the 5090 and PPU at `gs=32`. The direct PPU comparison remains authoritative for PPU.

This is strictly **SCALE_FIRST**: `gemv_lowbit` reads pre-materialized fp16 scale and zero planes. It does not consume native GGUF scales. `FULLY_QUANTIZED × GEMV_MOE` remains empty in `schemes.py`; this measurement scopes the potential structural benefit of building that missing kernel, not its native-scale overhead.
